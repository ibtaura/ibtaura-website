What this attention test measures

Attention isn't one skill. This test checks three parts of it, each with a short version of a task that researchers have used for decades.

Sustained attention: the reaction part is a vigilance task like the psychomotor vigilance task (PVT). You wait for a dot that comes at random moments and tap as fast as you can. Its 45-second length follows the brief PVT-B that Basner and colleagues validated in 2011. Slow taps and taps before the dot are what tiredness changes first.

Working memory: the digit memory part is a digit span task. A row of digits shows for a moment and you type it back. Every right answer adds a digit, so the longest row you get right is your span: how much you can hold in mind at once.

Cognitive flexibility: the rule switch part is a task-switching test. The rule changes between odd or even, lower or higher than a number, and whether the number is a multiple of 3. Switching costs time, as Monsell described in his 2003 review. It shows how quickly you can let go of one rule and pick up another.

Together they give a quick picture of how alert you are right now, not a fixed trait. Sleep, stress, time of day and caffeine all move these numbers from one day to the next.

How the score works

Each part gets its own score, and the Attention score combines them: 40% reaction, 30% memory and 30% rules. All of them use the scale the Clarus Health app uses, where 100 is typical and 15 points is one standard deviation. About two people in three score between 85 and 115, and a score of 130 is higher than about 98% of people.

The comparison values are beta norms taken from published studies of browser tests. Every run on the leaderboard is stored, so the norms will be re-fitted from real plays as more people take part, and scores may shift a little when that happens.

Phones and computers differ by a few milliseconds in how fast a tap or key press reaches the page, so the test records which kind of device you used. Reaction times in any browser also include a little screen and input delay, so compare yourself on the same device.

Tips for a fair score

Remove distractions: close other tabs, silence notifications and sit somewhere quiet for two minutes.

Use one device: on a computer, use the Space bar and the F and J keys. On a phone, hold it steady and tap with one thumb.

Don't guess the dot: taps before the dot count as too soon. Too many of them, or fewer than 8 dots answered, and the run can't be scored.

Play at different times: try it after a good night and after a short one, or before and after coffee, and see how your score moves.

How Clarus Health uses this

This test is a shortened version of the Quick Check in the Clarus Health app. In the app, the check takes a few minutes each morning, and 100 means your own normal rather than a typical player. Read alongside your sleep and the band's heart readings, a slower morning than usual is one of the early signs Clarus watches for when it looks at burnout risk.
