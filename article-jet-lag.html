Jet Lag and Resetting Your Body Clock

A long flight can move you across half the planet in an afternoon. Your body cannot follow at that speed. Deep inside the brain, a small cluster of cells keeps time for nearly every organ you have, and it adjusts to a new time zone slowly, roughly an hour or so a day. Jet lag is what that delay feels like: a body still living on the old schedule while the clock on the wall insists on another.

Most people think of jet lag as tiredness, the price of a missed night on a plane. Tiredness is part of it, but the deeper problem is timing. Sleep, digestion, hormone release, body temperature and alertness all follow daily rhythms, and those rhythms are set by an internal clock that expects the sun to rise and set at familiar hours. Cross several time zones and that expectation is suddenly wrong.

1. One Master Clock, Many Followers

The body's central timekeeper sits in the hypothalamus, in a pair of tiny structures called the suprachiasmatic nuclei, just above the point where the optic nerves cross. These cells run an approximately 24-hour cycle on their own, driven by a loop of genes that switch each other on and off over the course of a day. Left entirely alone, in constant dim light, the human clock tends to run slightly longer than 24 hours.

Because it does not run at exactly 24 hours, the master clock needs correcting every day. Its most powerful correcting signal is light. Specialised cells in the retina, distinct from those used for vision, respond especially to bright, blue-rich light and send that information straight to the master clock. Morning light tells the clock it is morning; darkness in the evening allows the hormone melatonin to rise and prepares the body for sleep.

The peripheral clocks

The master clock is not the only one. Almost every tissue in the body, including the liver, gut, pancreas, muscle and fat, carries its own molecular clock. These peripheral clocks time local jobs: when the liver is primed to process nutrients, when the gut is most active, when muscles are best able to use fuel. They take broad direction from the master clock through nerve signals, hormones and body temperature, but they also respond strongly to their own cues, especially the timing of meals and physical activity.

In ordinary life, these clocks run in step, like an orchestra following a single conductor.

2. Out of Step: What Crossing Time Zones Does

When you land in a new time zone, the local light-dark cycle changes instantly. Your internal clock does not. For the first days, your body is still running on home time: releasing melatonin, lowering core temperature and dampening alertness at hours that may now fall in the middle of a business meeting, while staying wide awake at what is locally the middle of the night.

This is the first layer of the problem, a mismatch between the internal clock and the external world. The second layer is less obvious. The master clock and the peripheral clocks do not shift at the same speed. The brain's clock responds most directly to light, while the liver and gut respond more to when you eat. For a period after travel, different organs can be keeping different times, a state researchers describe as internal desynchrony.

Jet lag is not simply a lack of sleep. It is a body whose organs are, for a few days, keeping different times from the world and from each other.

3. Why Eastward Travel Is Usually Harder

Most travellers notice that flying east is more punishing than flying west, and the physiology supports that impression. Travelling west lengthens your day. You arrive, and bedtime comes later than your body expects, so you need to delay your clock. Travelling east shortens the day. Bedtime arrives early, and you need to advance your clock instead.

Because the human clock tends naturally to run a little longer than 24 hours, it finds delaying easier than advancing. Staying up a few hours later is something most people do without much effort; falling asleep several hours earlier than usual is much harder, and waking early in a dark morning harder still. As a result, the clock usually adjusts more quickly after westward flights than after eastward ones.

When the clock goes the wrong way

On long eastward journeys, crossing many time zones, the body sometimes shifts in the opposite direction, delaying around the globe rather than advancing. This can happen when light exposure falls at the wrong point in the internal day. The effect is a slower, more muddled adjustment, and it is one reason the timing of light matters as much as the amount.

4. How Long It Takes to Adjust

A widely used rule of thumb holds that the body clock adjusts at roughly one time zone per day. A six-hour shift might therefore take around six days before sleep, appetite and energy feel fully settled. In practice, westward adjustment is often somewhat faster than this and eastward adjustment somewhat slower.

Individual differences are considerable. Adjustment tends to slow with age, and early risers and night owls may handle different directions with different ease. Sleep debt, alcohol, dehydration and travel stress all add to the load. On very short trips across a few time zones, staying close to home time can be the more comfortable choice.

5. What Jet Lag Feels Like

The most familiar symptoms involve sleep. After eastward travel, people often struggle to fall asleep at the new local bedtime and then find mornings heavy and slow. After westward travel, the more typical pattern is drowsiness in the early evening and waking far too early, wide awake before dawn. In both directions, sleep tends to be lighter and more broken than usual.

Digestion and appetite

Because the gut and liver keep their own time, digestive symptoms are common. Hunger may arrive at odd hours or not at all. Some travellers notice indigestion, constipation or general discomfort, as the digestive system receives food at times it was not prepared for.

Mood and thinking

Irritability, low mood and a general sense of flatness are frequent companions, as are difficulties with concentration, memory and reaction time. Alertness naturally dips at certain points in the internal day, and after travel those dips can land at inconvenient local hours. Decisions can feel effortful, and small tasks take longer than they should.

Physical performance

Strength, coordination and endurance also follow daily rhythms. Until the clock settles, physical effort can feel harder than usual.

6. Resetting the Clock: Light, Darkness, Food, Movement and Caffeine

No method makes adjustment instant, but several everyday signals can help the clock move in the right direction rather than drift. The principle behind all of them is the same: give the body clear, consistent cues about the new local time, and give them at the moments when the clock is most responsive.

Light and darkness

Light is the strongest lever by a wide margin. In broad terms, light in the late evening and the first part of the biological night tends to delay the clock, while light in the biological early morning tends to advance it. After westward travel, which calls for a delay, afternoon and evening daylight is usually helpful. After eastward travel, which calls for an advance, morning daylight usually helps, though after a very large eastward shift it can be wiser to wait until later in the morning for the first few days, so that bright light does not fall on the wrong side of the body's internal low point.

Darkness matters just as much. Dim lighting and fewer bright screens in the hours before the intended bedtime allow melatonin to rise on schedule, and a dark, cool bedroom supports deeper sleep. Avoiding bright light at the wrong time is as important as seeking it at the right one.

Light is the clock's most powerful signal, but it works in both directions. When you see it determines whether it helps you adjust or holds you back.

Meal timing

Eating on local time, rather than snacking whenever hunger strikes, gives the peripheral clocks in the gut and liver a clear signal. A substantial breakfast at the new local morning can be a useful anchor, while large meals late in the local night may work against adjustment. Some travellers begin shifting meals toward the destination schedule on the day of travel.

Movement

Physical activity is a gentler time cue than light or food, but it adds to the message, and a daytime walk outdoors pairs it with daylight. Vigorous exercise very close to the new bedtime can make sleep harder for some people.

Caffeine

Caffeine can support alertness during an adjustment, but its timing matters. It lingers in the body for hours, so caffeine taken in the afternoon or evening can make it harder to sleep at the new local bedtime and slow overall adjustment. Many people find it most useful early in the local day. Anyone considering supplements or medications to help with sleep across time zones should discuss them with a healthcare professional rather than improvising.

7. Watching the Clock Realign on a Wearable

Jet lag has traditionally been judged by feel alone: whether you slept, whether you are hungry at lunchtime, whether the afternoon slump has passed. Continuous wearable tracking adds another view. Several signals a wearable records follow daily rhythms of their own, and watching them over a trip can show the internal clock gradually moving toward local time.

Sleep is the most obvious. In the first nights after travel, sleep onset may come late or early relative to local time, wake-ups may be more frequent and total sleep may be shorter. Over the following days, sleep timing typically drifts toward the local schedule and becomes more consolidated.

Resting heart rate offers a quieter signal. Heart rate usually falls to its lowest point during sleep, and the timing and depth of that overnight low reflect both rest and the body's internal rhythm. After travel, many people see a higher overnight resting heart rate for a few nights, alongside lower heart rate variability, a pattern consistent with the strain of disrupted sleep, long flights and an unsettled schedule. As adjustment proceeds, both measures often drift back toward their usual range.

Skin temperature can be especially telling. As the body prepares for sleep, blood flow shifts toward the skin of the hands and feet and core temperature begins to fall, so peripheral skin temperature tends to rise through the night. That nightly curve is tied to the internal clock, and after a time-zone change it may initially appear out of position relative to local bedtime, then slide gradually into line.

Aura Clarus builds a long-term personal baseline for measures like these, so a stretch of travel can be read against what is normal for you rather than against a general average. The value lies less in any single night than in the trend: seeing several signals converge on their familiar pattern is a reasonable sign that your body has caught up with the clock on the wall.

8. The Bigger Picture

Jet lag is a vivid demonstration of something easy to forget in daily life: the body is not organised around a single moment, but around a rhythm. Nearly every system in it anticipates the day ahead, preparing for sleep before bedtime, for food before meals and for activity before waking. That anticipation is efficient when the day is predictable and disruptive when it suddenly is not.

The same principle applies beyond air travel. Late weekend nights followed by early Monday alarms, irregular meals and evenings under bright screens ask the clock to absorb smaller, repeated shifts, a kind of social jet lag without leaving home.

Seen this way, recovering from a long trip is less about enduring a few bad days and more about helping a finely tuned system find its footing. Consistent light, regular meals, daytime movement and sensible caffeine habits support the clock not only after a flight but every ordinary day. Paying attention to how your own body responds, through how you feel and through the trends in your sleep and physiology, is one of the more practical ways to keep that rhythm steady.
